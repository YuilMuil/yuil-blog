It is rather disheartening to see the impact frontier AI models are having on disciplines that people have traditionally taken pride in precisely because they were intellectually challenging.

This is becoming increasingly visible in theoretical fields such as computer science, mathematics, and physics. Since the release of Mythos a few months ago, we have begun seeing novel results at a pace that would have seemed extraordinary not long ago. Problems that might once have been considered award-worthy achievements[1] are increasingly becoming part of the "new normal." Even 25 year old conjectures that resisted attempts by many extremely capable researchers are now being solved with surprisingly little fanfare.[2]

The same transition is occurring in my own field: software engineering and vulnerability research. Frontier models are now discovering serious and, in some cases, critical vulnerabilities in complex, non-trivial software products.[4][5] Tasks that previously demanded substantial expertise in reverse engineering, program analysis, and systems security are becoming increasingly accessible to AI systems.

Despite this, I do not think the sciences will be "replaced" by AI in the near future. That would likely require another qualitative leap: systems capable not merely of solving problems that are presented to them, but of autonomously identifying important research directions, deciding which problems are worth pursuing, and sustaining that process without substantial human guidance. Whether that requires what we would call ASI or something beyond it is debatable, but I do not think we are there yet.

My perspective comes primarily from vulnerability research. I have found a fair number of vulnerabilities myself, both with and without AI assistance. Based on that experience, I suspect AI will increasingly eliminate much of the *process* that traditionally made up vulnerability research. The scarce human contribution will shift toward problem definition, target selection, methodology, result interpretation, and deciding what should be investigated in the first place.

Current frontier models are already remarkably capable, but they still require a surprising amount of hand-holding. They frequently pursue locally promising approaches that ultimately lead nowhere, continue investigating weak hypotheses for too long, or fail to recognize when a change in direction is necessary. Agent harnesses, iterative loops, explicit goals, and mechanisms such as `/goal` have mitigated some of these problems, but manual intervention is still often useful. A researcher may need to redirect the model, challenge an assumption, change the abstraction level, or suggest a completely different attack surface.

This distinction matters because vulnerability research has never been solely about mechanically finding bugs.

One of my former mentors described hacking as being fundamentally about ideas: breaking systems in ways their designers did not expect. I still think that is largely true. AI can already discover vulnerabilities with surprisingly little domain knowledge or human creativity, but only using it often produces variants, duplicates, or bugs in attack surfaces that other researchers have already exhausted.

Good vulnerability researchers develop their own methodologies and preferences over time. They learn which targets are interesting, which assumptions are likely to break, which components become dangerous when composed, and which seemingly unrelated subsystems might create unexpected trust boundaries when they intersect. There is also an element of taste: recognizing that an obscure behavior is not merely strange, but potentially indicative of a deeper security property being violated.

I do not think even the strongest frontier models reproduce the combination of methodology, target selection, intuition, and creativity today. They can execute an investigation extraordinarily well once pointed in a productive direction, but there remains a meaningful difference between exploring a search space and deciding which search spaces are worth creating in the first place.

That distinction may eventually disappear. If we reach systems capable of independently forming high-quality research agendas, generating genuinely novel methodologies, evaluating their significance, abandoning unproductive directions, and continuously generating better questions without external prompting, then the human role could shrink considerably, if not entirely. Whether that point should be called AGI, ASI, or something else is mostly a matter of terminology.

But until then, I suspect the nature of technical research will change more than it disappears.

For vulnerability research specifically, the shift may be somewhat bittersweet. Many people fell in love with reverse engineering and vulnerability research(including myself) because of the intellectual challenge involved in manually understanding complicated systems, experimenting with them, and eventually discovering something nobody else had noticed. As AI absorbs more of that execution and experimentation, some of that experience will inevitably become less valuable as a scarce skill.

The field may increasingly become a world in which the important human contribution is not performing every step of the discovery process, but generating the ideas that determine what gets discovered: choosing unusual intersections, constructing new methodologies, identifying overlooked assumptions, defining the right problems, and interpreting what the resulting evidence actually means.

For someone who primarily enjoys the mechanics of reverse engineering or the satisfaction of manually discovering a difficult bug, that future may be disappointing.

For someone who enjoys hacking because it is fundamentally about finding unexpected ways to break assumptions, however, there may still be plenty left to do.

At least for now...

Of course, this is simply my own perspective from working in the field, and I could easily be wrong about where the boundary ultimately settles <3
